To understand a Floating Gate Transistor, start with a standard MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor), which acts as a basic electronic switch.

A standard MOSFET has three terminals: a Source, a Drain, and a Control Gate (CG) that turns the current flow between source and drain on or off.

Flash memory adds a brilliant twist: it inserts a second gate—the Floating Gate (FG)—completely isolated between the Control Gate and the silicon channel.


The Insulation: The floating gate is made of polysilicon and is completely surrounded by high-quality insulators (usually silicon dioxide, $SiO_2$).
The "Floating" Part: Because it is electrically isolated, any electrons placed on the floating gate are trapped there. They cannot leak out, even when you turn the power off. 
This is what makes Flash memory non-volatile.

The presence or absence of trapped electrons on the floating gate changes how the transistor behaves when we try to read it.
It all comes down to the Threshold Voltage ($V_{th}$)—the minimum voltage required on the Control Gate to make the silicon channel conduct electricity.

Quantum Tunneling (Fowler-Nordheim Tunneling)In classical physics, if an electron does not have enough energy to jump over an energy barrier (the insulator), it simply cannot pass.In quantum mechanics, electrons behave as wavefunctions. Because of the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, there is a non-zero probability that an electron can "exist" on the other side of a thin barrier, effectively teleporting through it. This is called Quantum Tunneling.In Flash memory, we use Fowler-Nordheim (FN) Tunneling to erase (and sometimes write) data:A high voltage (around 10V to 20V) is applied to the Control Gate relative to the silicon substrate.This massive voltage creates a strong electric field ($E$) that severely bends the energy bands of the thin insulating oxide layer.The physical "width" of the barrier that the electron wave needs to cross becomes incredibly narrow.Electrons tunnel straight through the forbidden energy gap of the $SiO_2$ layer and land safely on the floating gate.The FN tunneling current density ($J_{FN}$) is highly sensitive to the electric field ($E$) and is governed by the following relation:$$J_{FN} = \alpha E^2 \exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{E}\right)$$Where:$E$ is the electric field across the oxide barrier.$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are physical constants determined by the effective mass of the electron and the barrier height ($\Phi_B$) at the silicon-oxide interface.Because of the exponential term, a small increase in voltage causes a massive surge in tunneling current, allowing rapid programming and erasing.Hot Carrier Injection (HCI)In some Flash designs (especially older NOR flash), electrons are pushed onto the gate using Hot Carrier Injection instead of pure tunneling:A high voltage is applied to both the Drain and the Control Gate.This accelerates electrons along the channel at extreme speeds, giving them high kinetic energy (making them "hot").These high-energy electrons collide with the silicon lattice near the drain and are deflected upward, gaining enough energy to physically leap over the energy barrier of the oxide layer, landing in the floating gate.4. The Engineering Limit: Why Flash Wears OutEvery time we force electrons through the insulating oxide layer using high-voltage tunneling, the physical structure of the oxide ($SiO_2$) degrades.Oxide Wear: Over thousands of write/erase cycles, the high electric fields break atomic bonds, creating "traps" (imperfections) in the oxide.Quantum Leakage: Eventually, these traps form a conductive path. Electrons trapped on the floating gate can tunnel back out on their own. The cell can no longer hold a charge, resulting in data corruption. This is why Flash drives have a finite lifespan (usually 3,000 to 100,000 write cycles).The Modern Transition: Because floating gates became so small that they could no longer reliably hold their charge without quantum-mechanical leakage, modern 3D NAND flash has largely transitioned to Charge Trap Flash (CTF). Instead of a conductive floating gate, CTF uses an insulating silicon nitride layer that holds electrons in localized, atomic-sized quantum wells, making the memory highly resistant to single-point defects.
